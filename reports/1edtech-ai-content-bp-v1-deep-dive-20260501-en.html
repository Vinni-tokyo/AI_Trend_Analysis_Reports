📊 Five Core Components of v1.0

This report dissects the 1EdTech AI-Generated Content Best Practices v1.0 document into five core components, detailing the substance of each, how they apply, and the intended use scenarios.

1. Five Guiding Principles

Status: v1.0 names five foundational principles that anchor every subsequent recommendation. They are not slogans — each maps directly to the Eight Best Practice Recommendations as actionable guidance.

Five Principles Defined

• Transparency: Clear disclosure of GenAI involvement and human oversight levels
• Accountability: Human experts validate outputs for accuracy and appropriateness
• Fairness & Non-Discrimination: Intentional review to prevent inequitable outcomes
• Accessibility: Compliance with WCAG, EN 301 549, and WAI-ARIA standards
• Trust: Strengthened through provenance tracking and SME review

Operational Mapping to Recommendations

• Transparency ↔ Recs 1 & 8: Labeling + public policy
• Accountability ↔ Rec 4: Mandatory SME review
• Fairness ↔ Rec 5: Bias audits + assessment validation
• Accessibility ↔ Rec 5: Alt-text, captions, assistive-tech compatibility
• Trust ↔ Recs 2 & 6: Provenance + metadata persistence

🔗 Related Links: v1.0 Official Document | WCAG 2.1 | WAI-ARIA
📊 Credibility Assessment: 9.5/10
Strengths: All five principle names are directly cited from the v1.0 official document; mapping aligns with the document's structure
Weaknesses: v1.0 does not explicitly state weighted priorities among the principles, so the mapping is the analyst's interpretation

Interpretation: The five principles share core values with the EU AI Act, NIST AI RMF, and OECD AI Principles, but they are framed in education-specific language (SME review, alt-text) — an important differentiator.

2. Eight Best Practice Recommendations

Status: The most action-oriented part of v1.0. The five principles are translated into eight concrete instructions applicable to publishers, educators, and EdTech vendors alike.

Eight Recommendations (Part 1)

• ① Transparency & Labeling: Mark GenAI contributions; ensure portability across platforms
• ② Provenance Tracking: Document model version, dates, human oversight, tokens used
• ③ Risk-Based Oversight: Differentiate high/medium/low stakes
• ④ Subject Matter Expert Review: Validate accuracy, rigor, standards alignment

Eight Recommendations (Part 2)

• ⑤ Bias & Accessibility Checks: Fairness audits + alt-text, captions, assistive-tech testing
• ⑥ Metadata & Storage: Retain with content, version control, audit trails
• ⑦ Standards Alignment: Use QTI, Common Cartridge, LTI, Caliper
• ⑧ Public GenAI-Use Statements: Organizations publish disclosure policies

🔗 Related Links: v1.0 Summary | 1EdTech Standards
📊 Credibility Assessment: 9.0/10
Strengths: All eight recommendation names and core descriptions are verifiable in the official document
Weaknesses: v1.0 does not specify priorities or normative levels (MUST/SHOULD/MAY) for each recommendation

Interpretation: The eight recommendations are arranged from micro (① Labeling) to macro (⑧ Public Policy). Recommendation ⑦ (Standards Alignment) is a unique differentiator only possible because 1EdTech is itself a standards consortium — a feature absent from NIST and EU AI Act guidance.

3. Five-Tier Transparency Labeling Framework

Status: The flagship instrument operationalizing Recommendation ①. Instead of a binary "AI used / not used" indicator, it quantifies the degree of human control across five tiers. Labels are embedded in QTI/Common Cartridge metadata so they persist across system migrations.

Tier Name Human vs AI Share Representative Use Case High-Stakes Use
L1 Human-Created, GenAI-Assisted Human 95% / AI 5% Spell-check, formatting, synonym suggestions ✅ Recommended
L2 Human-Led, GenAI-Enhanced Human 75% / AI 25% Human controls pedagogy, AI refines expression ✅ Recommended
L3 GenAI-Co-Created, Human-Guided Human 50% / AI 50% AI drafts, expert reviews and restructures ⚠️ Conditional
L4 GenAI-Dominant, Human-Supervised Human 25% / AI 75% AI generates most content, human makes minimal edits ⚠️ Discouraged
L5 Fully AI-Generated Human ≤5% / AI ≥95% AI autonomously produces with minimal oversight ❌ Not recommended in high-stakes domains
🔗 Related Links: QTI v3 Implementation Guide | Common Cartridge
📊 Credibility Assessment: 9.0/10
Strengths: Five-tier names and definitions are directly cited from the v1.0 official document
Weaknesses: The "Human vs AI Share" percentages are this report's visual interpretation and are not stated in v1.0

Interpretation: The Level 5 "not recommended for high-stakes" guidance applies to assessments, credentialing, and diagnostics — content that directly affects learner outcomes. For supplementary materials (summaries, translations, study tips), Level 5 remains acceptable.

4. Risk-Based Oversight Model + Subject-Specific Considerations

Status: The operational form of Recommendation ③. Content is classified into three risk tiers based on impact on learner outcomes, with additional verification items called out across five academic disciplines.

Three Risk Tiers + Validation Intensity

• High Risk: Grades, placement, credentialing → multiple SME reviews, compliance documentation, quantitative accuracy metrics required
• Medium Risk: Practice items, formative assessment → single SME review, sample-based consistency check
• Low Risk: Supplementary, translation, drafting → automated validation, post-hoc monitoring

Five Discipline-Specific Checks

• Mathematics: Equation formatting, step-by-step solution accuracy
• Languages: Linguistic precision, cultural sensitivity, target-language fidelity
• Science: Cross-check against authoritative sources, terminology consistency
• Social Studies: Fact-checking, multiple perspectives, bias mitigation
• Other Fields: Discipline-appropriate SME review, technical accuracy and authenticity verification

🔗 Related Links: TrustEd Apps GenAI Data Rubric | 1EdTech
📊 Credibility Assessment: 9.0/10
Strengths: The three risk tiers and five disciplines are all explicitly named in the v1.0 document
Weaknesses: Operational definitions like "multiple SME review" vs "single SME review" are this report's interpretation

Interpretation: v1.0 deliberately separates risk and discipline. Even within "high-risk," math (equation accuracy) and social-studies (multiple perspectives) require different validation. The intended usage is to operate the two axes (risk × discipline) as a matrix.

5. Four Content Categories + Metadata + Accessibility + Academic Integrity (Integrated Guidance)

Status: The latter half of v1.0 integrates accountability distribution by content creator (four categories) with operational infrastructure (metadata, accessibility, academic integrity). This is the final piece that makes the guidance operationally feasible.

Accountability by Four Categories

• Publisher-Produced: Full provenance, risk assessment, bias audits, SME validation, standards alignment all required
• Educator-Produced: Classroom disclosure, brief provenance, teacher validation, metadata retention
• Student-Produced: Student disclosure of AI contribution, careful review for high-stakes work, alignment with academic integrity
• Hybrid: Multi-point disclosure, dual provenance, stricter oversight standards applied

Metadata · Accessibility · Integrity

• Required Metadata Elements: Model, version, date, training-data reuse, human oversight, audit trail, SME records
• Accessibility Standards: WCAG 2.1 AA + Section 508 + EAA + Accessible Canada Act
• Accessibility Validation: AI-generated alt-text, caption accuracy, assistive-tech compatibility testing
• Academic Integrity: Role-appropriate disclosure obligations for publishers, educators, and students

🔗 Related Links: Section 508 | Accessible Canada Act | European Accessibility Act
📊 Credibility Assessment: 9.0/10
Strengths: The four categories, seven metadata elements, and four accessibility standards are all stated in v1.0
Weaknesses: Operational examples for the student-produced category (university-specific policies) need follow-up sources beyond v1.0

Interpretation: The student-disclosure obligation is v1.0's most novel contribution. Publisher and educator accountability is well-trodden territory in EdTech, but explicitly naming students as accountable parties is rare and likely to trigger redefinitions of academic operations.

Five Key Shifts Emphasized by v1.0

• Binary → Five Tiers: From "AI used or not" to a precise five-tier quantification of GenAI involvement
• Uniform → Risk-Tiered: From applying identical validation to all content to differentiated governance by risk tier
• Volatile → Persistent Metadata: AI label loss across systems is solved by integration into QTI/CC/LTI/Caliper
• Vendor Accountability → Student Inclusion: AI disclosure obligations now extend beyond publishers and teachers to students
• Accessibility as Add-On → Integrated Validation: WCAG, EAA, and Section 508 are embedded in the AI content validation workflow

📋 Executive Summary

Key Insights

  • Five Principles Anchor the Eight Recommendations: Abstract values (Transparency, Accountability, Fairness, Accessibility, Trust) are translated into eight actionable instructions, providing the EdTech industry's first operationally feasible AI governance guide.
  • Five-Tier Labels Are the Most Innovative Element: Shifting from binary AI marking to five-tier quantification is v1.0's most distinctive contribution, mapping naturally to EU AI Act Article 50.
  • Three-Axis Matrix Drives Operational Value: The five-tier labels × four categories × three risk tiers must be operated together. Applying only one axis materially limits effectiveness.
  • Students Elevated as Disclosure Parties: The recommendation that students — not just publishers and teachers — disclose AI contributions requires academic-integrity policy redesigns.
  • Standards Alignment Is the Differentiator: Recommendation ⑦ (QTI/CC/LTI/Caliper integration) is a unique advantage rooted in 1EdTech's standards-consortium identity, absent from NIST or EU guidance.

Adoption Priorities by Scenario

① Assessment & Credentialing Providers → Five-Tier Labels + Multi-SME Validation First
Rationale: High-stakes content dominates these portfolios; immediate adoption of Recs ①·④ delivers the largest risk-mitigation impact.
② LMS / LXP Vendors → Recommendation ⑦ (Standards Alignment) + Rec ⑥ (Metadata) First
Rationale: Frequent cross-system content movement makes persistent metadata the key differentiator.
③ Universities & Schools → Four Categories + Academic-Integrity Policy First
Rationale: The student-disclosure mandate drives the largest operational change.
④ Content Publishers → Full Eight Recommendations + Discipline-Specific SME Matrix
Rationale: The publisher category bears the heaviest accountability burden in v1.0, warranting phased full adoption.

Overall Assessment: Highly Positive (v1.0 is the EdTech industry's first comprehensive guide that translates abstract principles into eight executable recommendations and a three-axis matrix. Its non-normative posture lowers adoption friction and is likely to accelerate diffusion.)

📖 Key Terminology

1EdTech Consortium: Global non-profit consortium that publishes EdTech interoperability standards. Formerly known as IMS Global Learning Consortium.
Informative (Non-Normative) Document: Advisory guidance with no certification or compliance requirements. Adoption is selective based on institutional context.
Five-Tier Transparency Labels: v1.0's flagship instrument quantifying GenAI involvement from Human-Created to Fully AI-Generated across five tiers.
Risk-Based Oversight: A governance model that calibrates validation intensity according to a content's impact on learner outcomes.
SME (Subject Matter Expert): Domain expert responsible for verifying the academic accuracy and pedagogical appropriateness of AI-generated content.
Provenance: Metadata tracking content creation history, including model name, version, date, human oversight, and SME records.
