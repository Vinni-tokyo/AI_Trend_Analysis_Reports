What Separates Look-Alike Career AI Is Where Its Input Comes From
— And Nobody Publishes How Theirs Is Built

Lay out the landscape in six lineages and systems that looked identical split on what they are entitled to claim
Report date: 26 October 2026 | Standard report (5-8 pages) | Data collected: 26 October 2026

Top 5 Trends

The argument of this report
AI that asks about your career, names your skill gaps and hands you a learning path all looks alike from the outside. Lay the landscape out in six lineages, though, and it is not features but "what grounds the claim" that defines the category — a self-reported résumé, a platform's behavioural record, a credential verified by an issuer, or a national database. And we could not find a single product in this field that publishes a model card or a system card. The final section argues this is because regulation aims at the employer side and passes career advice by.

1. What exists out there — six lineages

Where things stand: This is not one market but six lineages with different origins meeting on the same screen. General-purpose chat models come down from above, job platforms come in from the side, and governments are building up from below.

Four lineages from the private sector

• ① General-purpose chat models: they do career counselling with no dedicated feature. Their only grounds are what the user told them
• ② Career exploration experiments: widening the view to adjacent roles and possibilities. Google's Career Dreamer sits here
• ③ Job-matching: connecting résumés to postings — LinkedIn, Teal, Korea's JobKorea and Wanted, Japan's doda and Miidas
• ④ Enterprise talent intelligence (B2B): mapping skills inside an organisation — Gloat, Eightfold

Learning and public, two more

• ⑤ Starting from a learning platform: coming down from role to learning path to credential — Coursera Career Academy
• ⑥ 🔴 National skills passports: the most striking lineage in this report. Singapore, the Philippines and Saudi Arabia are already live or launched, California is exploring, and the UK is changing who operates its service
• The private sector sells "a better recommendation"; the state builds "the verified record itself" — they are not competitors but different layers

🔗 Related Links: LinkedIn | Teal | Gloat | Eightfold AI | Coursera Career Academy | SkillsFuture

Outlook: Because all six meet on one screen, users read them as the same thing. But to pick out "systems like ours" you have to look at the origin, not the feature list. The third section sets out how to judge that.

📊 Credibility Assessment: 7/10
Strengths: Every product and programme listed is confirmed to exist in public material. The five national passport cases rest on government announcements and institutional sources.
Gaps: 🔴 The "six lineages" classification is this report's proposal, not an established market segmentation. The list is also not exhaustive — China, India and Latin America were not surveyed, and for the Japanese cases much of what is online is affiliate comparison content, so official specifications could not always be confirmed.

2. Governments are moving in — five national skills passports

Where things stand: Most existing discussion covers private platforms. The striking movement in 2026 is governments building "a verified record of skills" as public infrastructure, with career AI layered on top.

Live or launched

• Singapore — SkillsFuture Skills Passport: a free verified profile holding skills, education and work history, with an "Insights" feature to explore career paths and plan skill development
• Philippines — TESDA Skills Passport: a mobile platform bringing training, verified credentials, scholarships, career information and employment into one system. Its AI career tool, My Career Advisor, comes from a partnership with the Wadhwani Foundation
• Saudi Arabia — AI Skills Passport: launched in July 2026 by the Ministry of Communications and Information Technology with EY and Microsoft through the national eLearning platform FutureX (AI fundamentals, ethics, career planning)

Exploring or transferring

• California — Career Passport: a concept in the state's master plan, framed as a digital wallet for learners and workers
• UK — National Careers Service: offers skills assessments, career profiles and course search; the Department for Work and Pensions plans to bring operations in-house when contracts end in September 2027
• What they share is "free, verified, state-backed" — a combination the private sector struggles to offer all at once

🔗 Related Links: SkillsFuture Skills Passport | TESDA | AI Skills Passport (EY) | National Careers Service

Outlook: If public infrastructure takes on "the verified record", the competitive ground shifts for everyone else — from gathering the data to what you can say with it. That said, the five cases are at very different stages and differ in what they cover.

📊 Credibility Assessment: 6/10
Strengths: The Saudi item comes from the state news agency (SPA); the Philippine TESDA programme and the UK transfer are confirmed from institutional and public records. That the five cases span different regions and stages is also verified.
Gaps: 🔴 The Singapore description rests on commercial guidance material and may differ from official specifications. Actual user numbers and the specific behaviour of each AI feature were not verified. No material on an EU Europass equivalent was found — which means not confirmed in this survey, not that none exists.

3. What decides whether two systems are the same category is the origin of the input

Where things stand: Compare by feature list and almost every product has "career recommendations, skill-gap analysis, learning paths", so nothing separates. The real divisions run along five axes — and the first one largely determines the rest.

A count of the systems listed in this report, classified by the origin of their input. (A tally by the present analysis — not an exhaustive survey.)

Five axes for judging similarity

• ① Origin of the input: self-reported / platform behaviour / issuer-verified / national database
• ② Conversation or form: narrowed over several turns, or taken in one go and generated
• ③ Unit of recommendation: narrative and advice / courses / attainable credentials and badges / job postings
• ④ Artefact you take away: does a document such as a résumé or application paragraph remain
• ⑤ Who pays: individual subscription / enterprise / placement fee / public budget

Why ① pulls the others along

• Take self-reported input and there is no way to verify it, which makes it hard to narrow the recommendation unit to a credential → the product drifts toward narrative and advice
• Take issuer-verified credentials and it becomes countable — "M of the N you hold" — and the unit narrows to real, issuable things
• Take a national database and free, universal access becomes possible, at the price of speed and scope being bound to policy
• So choosing ① largely settles ③, ④ and ⑤ — when hunting for "a similar product", the first question is the input, not the features

🔗 Related Links: Open Badges — 1EdTech | 1EdTech Consortium
Read alongside — this site's 30 September 2026 report "AI Career Guidance Platforms: From Self-Report to Verified Evidence" looked at the major platforms one by one (LinkedIn, Career Dreamer, Gloat, Teal). This report puts the whole landscape and a test for similarity on top of that — not profiles, but "what should count as the same category".

Outlook: These five axes are usable as soon as you build a comparison table. Instead of adding feature columns, write ① first and read the rest against it, and why a product looks the way it does starts to explain itself.

📊 Credibility Assessment: 5/10
Strengths: The value on each axis — what a given product takes as input — is an observable property that public material mostly confirms.
Gaps: 🔴 The five axes and the claim that ① pulls the others along are entirely this report's proposal. No source offers this frame, and nothing here was tested statistically. Counter-examples are possible — a system drawing on a national database yet handing back no artefact, for one.

4. 🔴 There are no model cards — disclosure exists only where the law reaches

Where things stand: This survey found no career or hiring AI product in this field publishing a model card or a system card. But "nobody discloses anything" would be wrong. Disclosure does exist — in exactly the one place a law compels it, and even that one place has been judged incomplete on a full review.

What is still not disclosed

• Model cards and system cards: not one in this survey, and in some cases not even the name of the model in use
• The decision structure: what decides whether to ask, answer or decline — code or the model
• Provenance of training and retrieval data: which material the recommendation comes from. What gets published is scale only ("more than one billion anonymized career profiles") — not composition, consent or sourcing
• So material for comparing methodologies between products is still thin — those columns in a comparison table stay "unverified"

What is disclosed — the one place the law reaches

• New York City's Local Law 144 requires employers using automated employment decision tools to publish a summary of a bias audit online. 116 published audits were collected between the law taking effect (July 2023) and early November 2024
• A joint study by the ACLU, the University of Pennsylvania, Stanford and Harvard (FAccT 2025) analysed all 116 and concluded they are "incomplete evaluations of algorithmic bias" — missing demographic data, opaque aggregation, problematic uses of "test data", and metrics that do not represent how the tools are actually used
• One vendor (Eightfold) publishes those audit summaries and its fairness-testing method (impact ratio, perturbation across eight attributes) on its own pages, and is certified to ISO/IEC 42001:2023. It still publishes no model card, and the full audit documents are customer-only
• Another vendor (Gloat) has a responsible-AI page carrying no figures and no audit results

🔗 Related Links: Auditing the Audits (FAccT 2025) | Eightfold Responsible AI | The 2026 AI in Hiring Report — Greenhouse | ISO/IEC 42001
Read alongside — the 14 October 2026 report "The Scorekeepers Are Now Being Audited" covered a standard benchmark receiving a "Flawed" verdict after 24 defects in a 50-task sample. Overlay the two and the shape is clear — the model layer published its scores of its own accord and so became auditable; the product layer gets audited only in the one place a law compels it.

Outlook: Seen this way the shape of the gap is clear. Disclosure did not grow voluntarily; it appeared only where a law reached. So it remains vacant ground for whoever discloses first — and the final section explains why the law reaches only that far.

📊 Credibility Assessment: 7/10
Strengths: The full-population analysis of the 116 published audits was confirmed directly in the primary paper — "Auditing the Audits" (FAccT 2025), by the ACLU, the University of Pennsylvania, Stanford and Harvard; N=116, collected from the law taking effect in July 2023 to early November 2024. Eightfold's disclosed items (audit summaries, impact ratio, perturbation across eight attributes) and its ISO/IEC 42001:2023 certification, and the absence of figures on Gloat's page, were confirmed on each vendor's own pages.
Gaps: 🔴 "No model card found" is still not proof of absence — customer-only documentation could not be checked, and only two vendors' official pages were read in full. The 116 audits are a collection through early November 2024, so the current count may be higher. Scale figures such as "more than one billion" are the vendor's own account. (The first edition stated that evaluation metrics and third-party audits are also undisclosed; that was wrong and was corrected on 26 October 2026 — the 116 Local Law 144 audits and one vendor's published fairness testing exist.)

5. Regulation aims at the employer side and passes career advice by

Where things stand: The weak incentive to disclose has an institutional cause. The EU AI Act's high-risk list (Annex III) aims at the employment relationship — recruitment and selection, promotion and termination, task allocation, performance monitoring. A product advising an individual on their career tends to fall outside that.

What Annex III covers

• Recruitment and selection — targeted job advertising, filtering applications, evaluating candidates
• Decisions in the employment relationship — promotion and termination, task allocation, performance and behaviour monitoring
• Duties to inform: Article 26(7) requires employers to inform workers' representatives and affected workers before putting a high-risk system into service at the workplace; 26(11) adds that people subject to such a decision must be told
• Article 6(3) can exempt narrow procedural tasks such as interview scheduling — but the exemption never applies to a system that profiles people
• Emotion inference in the workplace is not high-risk but a prohibited practice

⚠️ Sources disagree on the timing

• Several sources give 2 December 2027 for Annex III obligations, describing an adjustment under the Digital Omnibus (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744)
• Others still say August 2026. The latter appear outdated, but this survey could not settle it
• Article 50 transparency duties, such as marking generated content, are a separate track and applied on schedule on 2 August 2026
• 🔴 For anything that goes into a business document, verify against the Official Journal text

🔗 Related Links: EU AI Act portal | EUR-Lex (Official Journal)

Outlook: The shape resolves like this. Products sold to employers carry duties to inform; products advising individuals do not. So methodology disclosure on the career-advice side remains a choice rather than a legal requirement. That explains why the gap in the previous section does not close on its own — and, by the same token, why whoever discloses voluntarily can make it a differentiator.

📊 Credibility Assessment: 6/10
Strengths: The scope of Annex III point 4, the duties in Article 26(7) and 26(11), the limit on the Article 6(3) exemption, and the prohibition on workplace emotion inference are consistent across several commentaries.
Gaps: 🔴 Article numbers and the application date were not verified against the legal text. Most of what was consulted were compliance blogs, and the sources disagree on timing (2 Dec 2027 vs Aug 2026). The judgement that career-advice products fall outside is this report's interpretation, not a legal opinion — and it can change the moment such a product is sold to an employer.

Five shifts in the market

  • Governments created a layer: Singapore, the Philippines and Saudi Arabia are live or launched, California is exploring, and the UK is changing who operates its service.
  • Feature lists no longer separate anyone: almost every product has career recommendations, skill-gap analysis and learning paths. The division runs along the origin of the input.
  • Disclosure exists only where the law reaches: not one product publishes a model card; what is published is the 116 bias-audit summaries New York City's Local Law 144 compels — and a full-population review judged those incomplete.
  • Regulation is asymmetric: products sold to employers carry duties to inform; products advising individuals do not.
  • The competitive ground is moving: as the state takes on the verified record, private players compete on what they can say with it.

Application Ideas

Approaches observed in the market.

1. Write the input origin first - in any comparison table, put "what grounds the claim" in the first column ahead of features and read the rest against it

2. Check for public infrastructure - find out whether the territory you operate in has a skills passport, and whether it can be integrated with

3. Audit what you could disclose - list which of decision structure, evaluation metrics and data provenance your own organisation could publish

Executive Summary

Key findings

  • The landscape has six lineages: general-purpose chat, career exploration experiments, job matching, enterprise talent intelligence, learning platforms, and national skills passports. Different origins behave differently even with identical features.
  • Governments have moved in: Singapore, the Philippines and Saudi Arabia are live or launched; California is exploring; the UK brings operations in-house in September 2027. "Free, verified, state-backed" is a combination private players struggle to offer at once.
  • What defines the category is the origin of the input: self-reported input pushes a product toward narrative and advice; issuer-verified credentials narrow it to real, attainable things. Choosing the input largely settles the rest.
  • No model cards, and disclosure only where the law reaches: New York City's Local Law 144 obliges employers to publish bias-audit summaries, which produced 116 public audits; the ACLU-led study (FAccT 2025) analysed all of them and concluded they are incomplete evaluations. On the career-advice side, which the law does not reach, no model or system card was found at all.
  • Asymmetric regulation keeps the incentive weak: EU AI Act Annex III aims at recruitment, selection, promotion and task allocation, and products advising individuals tend to fall outside it.

Reference points by situation

Doing competitive analysis → put input origin in the first column
Why: by feature list, nearly every product looks the same. With different inputs, the same feature works at a different level.
Looking at business in Asia → check public infrastructure first
Why: Singapore and the Philippines already hold verified records as public infrastructure. That can be a foundation rather than a competitor.
Making trust your differentiator → this is vacant ground
Why: not one model card was found, and the disclosure that does exist is confined to the one place a law compels it. Since the law does not demand it here, whoever goes first takes the position.

Overall assessment: Neutral. A widening landscape and the arrival of public infrastructure are signs this category is maturing. But methodology disclosure is effectively absent, so comparison between products stays at the level of feature lists, and asymmetric regulation means that will not improve by itself. With users short of material for judging what to trust, market maturity and trust maturity are not yet moving together.

Key Terminology

Skills passport — a public profile holding an individual's skills, education and work history in verified form. It is called a passport because the holder carries it and presents it.
Self-report — information the person wrote themselves, as in a résumé or profile. With no way to verify it, there is a ceiling on the precision of anything built on top.
Verified credential — a qualification or badge vouched for by an issuer. Because who issued it, when, and on what basis travels with it, it becomes a countable signal.
Model card · system card — public documents: a model card covers specifications and benchmark results, a system card covers pre-deployment safety evaluation. Neither was found among products in this field.
High-risk AI — the category the EU AI Act sets aside for uses that can materially affect people's rights or livelihoods, attaching extra obligations. Recruitment and selection fall here.
Annex III — the EU AI Act's list of high-risk uses. Point 4 covers employment and worker management.
Talent intelligence — the enterprise (B2B) field in which a company infers its own workforce's skills to inform placement, mobility and development. Its inputs and its payer both differ from a consumer career service.
