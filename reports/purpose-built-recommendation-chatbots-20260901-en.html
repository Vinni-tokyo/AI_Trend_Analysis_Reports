Top 5 Trends

1. Eval-driven development has become the de facto standard

Where things stand: As of 2026, writing the evaluation before writing the feature has settled into industry guidance. It is test-driven development adapted to non-deterministic systems: instead of asserting an exact output, you assert that scores on a labelled dataset stay above a threshold. Every change that touches a prompt, a model version, or a retrieval configuration triggers an eval run, and a change that regresses past the threshold does not merge.

The standard pipeline

• Golden dataset: 100–500 curated examples
• Task-specific metrics: 3–5 of them (not BLEU or ROUGE)
• LLM-as-judge layer: with human spot checks on 10% of judgements
• CI hook: blocks regressions beyond a 5% threshold

Why at least 100 cases

• Signal vs noise: the minimum size that separates a real regression from run-to-run variance
• Accumulated failures: every failure you have already seen becomes a fixed case
• Statistical significance: judging decimal-point movement requires a sample
• CI integration: a quality gate only matters if it runs automatically

Outlook: Evaluation assets are outliving prompts. Models get swapped but the eval set stays, which makes the eval set itself the team's compounding asset.

2. The LLM judge has matured — but the ruler itself moves

Where things stand: LLM-as-a-Judge scores, classifies, or compares another model's output, and the 2026 guidance is explicit: use a stronger model to judge a weaker one, pin the judge model version, use structured 1-to-5 rubrics rather than free-form scoring, and have a human spot-check 10% of judgements every release.

What must be pinned

• Model version: an auto-upgrade silently changes the ruler
• Region: the same model can behave differently by region
• Rubric hash: a changed rubric makes past figures incomparable
• Temperature: fixed at 0 for reproducibility

A measured judge effect

• Same data, different judge: mean 0.81 → 0.66
• Difference: −0.15 (noise ratio +11.1 pp)
• Run-to-run variance: −0.02
• Implication: the judge effect can exceed the product change being measured

Outlook: The gap is widening between teams that use a judge and teams that calibrate one. Without a golden set, swapping the judge invalidates every historical number at once.

📊 Credibility Assessment: 8.0/10
Strengths: Industry guidance drawn from primary sources such as DeepEval; the −0.15 judge effect is a measured figure from a real project
Weaknesses: That measurement comes from a single project, so generalising it needs further validation

3. RAG evaluation has split into two measurement points

Where things stand: The standard recommendation for a 2026 RAG evaluation programme is to track at least one retrieval-stage metric and at least one generation-stage metric. Tracking only generation hides retrieval regressions; tracking only retrieval misses fabrication. In one documented case a legal research system shipped with faithfulness at 0.91 yet missed key statutes in roughly one in six responses because context recall was 0.62.

Two distinct failure surfaces

• Retrieval miss: relevant chunks never surface (low recall)
• Retrieval noise: irrelevant chunks surface (low precision)
• Generation failure: the model answers coherently from partial context
• Debuggability: fine-tuned models collapse both surfaces into one, making causes hard to separate

Coverage gaps

• Definition: the query asks for a concept absent from the corpus
• Symptom: a correctly cautious but useless answer, with low proxy scores
• Misdiagnosis risk: easily mistaken for a retrieval-tuning problem
• Remedy: corpus auditing and gap-filling with canonical documents — not algorithms

Outlook: Diagnosing "the recommendations are poor" as a retrieval problem, an input problem, or an inventory problem is becoming standard practice. The three belong to different owners — engineering, data, and business respectively.

4. "Operational safety" has become an evaluation axis of its own

Where things stand: For purpose-built agents, the ability to refuse out-of-domain queries accurately while remaining helpful on in-domain ones is now called operational safety and is measured separately. The 2026 research suite OffTopicEval instantiates 21 purpose-specific agents and tests each one's ability to refuse and to accept correctly.

Layered guardrails

• Input guards: topic classification rejects off-topic queries
• Output guards: filter policy-violating language
• System guards: access control, citation requirements, data-loss prevention
• Example: a healthcare bot blocks financial advice; a support agent refuses political commentary

Common leak paths

• The "by the way" form: classification is correct but content slips in while writing
• Capability promises: "paste it in and I'll handle it"
• Incomplete notices: refusing without saying why or where to go
• Attribute-based judgement: sorting people by leaning or background

5. The skills ontology sets the ceiling on recommendation quality

Where things stand: The data foundation for career and learning recommendation is the skills taxonomy. Organisations either license a ready-made open taxonomy (Lightcast, ESCO, O*NET) or maintain a proprietary one (Eightfold's 1.6M skills, Workday Skills Cloud, Beamery's Talent Graph, Gloat's Loomra knowledge graph). On the credential side, Open Badges 3.0 was approved as a final standard in June 2024 with institutional adoption spreading through 2025–2026, though 2.0 remains the most widely used standard in practice.

The taxonomy landscape

• Lightcast: built from external labour-market signals — job postings, credentials, regional supply and demand
• O*NET: the US Department of Labor standard
• ESCO: the EU's multilingual classification
• TechWolf: AI-inferred, continuously refreshed skills

How skill inference is evolving

• Declared: what people write on a profile (low completeness)
• Inferred from history: skills derived from work history — Eightfold's edge
• Work signals: proficiency drawn from commits, output, tickets
• Verifiable credentials: Open Badges 3.0 has cryptographic signing built in

Outlook: A point arrives where securing the things to recommend constrains progress more than improving the recommender does. An occupation with an empty catalogue stays empty no matter how much retrieval improves.

Application Ideas

Approaches observed in the market.

1. Build a golden set and calibrate the judge - have humans label a stratified sample of 100–200 cases and track agreement with the judge as a standing metric

2. Publish a coverage map - state which occupations are supported and which are not, turning empty areas from a defect into a declared scope

3. Keep dual metrics permanently visible - place a retrieval-stage and a generation-stage metric on the same dashboard so regressions cannot hide

Executive Summary

Key findings

  • Evaluation is the asset: models and prompts get replaced, eval sets persist. Improvements accumulated without an eval set cannot be proven at the next model change.
  • A moving ruler makes improvement unprovable: in one measurement, swapping the judge alone moved the mean by 0.15 — larger than most product changes.
  • Recommendation failures come in three kinds: retrieval, input, and inventory — owned by engineering, data, and the business respectively.
  • Retrieval cannot invent what is absent: domains missing from the corpus are not fixed by better algorithms. The remedies are content acquisition or narrower scope.
  • Honesty outweighs accuracy: a user can judge a wrong recommendation, but has no way to check a consultation the system claims never happened.

Reference points by situation

Organisations that already have an eval system → consider building a golden set first
Rationale: without a floor to calibrate the judge against, accumulated figures are invalidated the moment the judge changes.
Organisations with large coverage gaps → consider narrowing scope as an option
Rationale: acquiring content takes time, and promising less in the meantime can be the better route to preserving trust.
Pre-launch organisations → weigh offline evaluation against online signal
Rationale: offline evaluation is strong at preventing regressions and weak at deciding what to build.

Overall sentiment: Positive — the evaluation methodology has reached maturity and the tooling ecosystem is in place. What remains is a non-technical constraint: securing data and content.

Key Terminology

Eval-Driven Development (EDD) - Writing the evaluation before the feature. Test-driven development applied to LLMs: it asserts score thresholds rather than exact outputs.
Golden Set - Human-labelled reference data. It is the floor against which judges and metrics are calibrated; at least 100 cases are recommended.
LLM-as-a-Judge - Using one LLM to score another's output. Pinning the model version and the rubric is a precondition.
Context Recall / Precision - Retrieval-stage metrics: how completely relevant documents were retrieved (recall) and how relevant the retrieved ones are (precision).
Faithfulness - A generation-stage metric measuring whether the answer is grounded in the retrieved documents. High faithfulness with low recall still misses the point.
Coverage Gap - A state where the concept the query needs is simply absent from the corpus. Better retrieval will not close it.
Operational Safety - The ability to refuse out-of-domain queries accurately while remaining useful on in-domain ones. A core metric for purpose-built agents.
Skills Ontology - A structured classification of occupations, skills and their relationships. Open standards such as Lightcast, ESCO and O*NET coexist with proprietary corporate taxonomies.
