Plenty of Books on Recommendation — What Is Missing Is a Definition
And the Closest One Comes Out in Two Weeks

Between the books that criticise recommendation and the books that teach you to build it, the seat that asks what recommendation is sits empty
Report date: 26 October 2026 | Standard report (5-8 pages) | Data collected: 26 October 2026

Top 5 Trends

The central claim of this report
There is no shortage of books about recommendation. But they fall into two clusters: books that criticise what recommendation has done to society, and books that teach you how to build one. Between them, the seat that asks "what is a recommendation?" and sorts recommendation into kinds is empty. This survey found no book that makes that question the central argument of a volume. More striking: the title that comes closest to that seat is due out in Japan on 10 November 2026.

1. There is effectively one trade book on recommendation

Where things stand: Search for "a book on recommender systems" and almost everything returned is a textbook or a practitioner's guide. A single volume a non-technical reader can pick up and read about recommendation as a subject in itself — this survey confirmed exactly one.

The one trade book confirmed

• Michael Schrage, Recommendation Engines (MIT Press, 1 September 2020, around 296 pp.)
• Essential Knowledge series — MIT Press's concept primers written for readers outside the field
• Contents: what recommenders are → the origins of recommendation (reaching back to antiquity's oracles and astrologers) → a brief history of recommendation engines → how recommenders work → experiencing recommendations → recommendation innovators → the recommender future
• Case studies: Spotify, ByteDance and Stitch Fix
• The closing question: will recommenders leave us disappointed and dependent, or help us discover the world and ourselves in novel and serendipitous ways?

Everything else is a textbook or a guide

• Recommender Systems Handbook (Springer) · Recommender Systems: Frontiers and Practices (Springer, 2024)
• Practical Recommender Systems for Work — Japanese: 『推薦システム実践入門』 (O'Reilly Japan, May 2022, 296 pp.), subtitled "a deployment guide you can use at work"
• 『基礎から学ぶ推薦システム』 (Corona Publishing) · Jannach, Japanese edition 『情報推薦システム入門』 (Kyoritsu, 2012)
• 🔴 In the Korean market this survey found no original work. The only recommender-systems volume confirmed, 추천 시스템 입문 (Hanbit Media, May 2023, 296 pp.), is a translation of the Japanese guide above — the authors and page count match

🔗 Related Links: Recommendation Engines — MIT Press | 推薦システム実践入門 — O'Reilly Japan | 추천 시스템 입문 — Kyobo Book

Outlook: That distribution does not mean there is no demand. Recommendation reaches us dozens of times a day, and exactly one trade book explains it. The saturated ground is not recommendation itself but the seat beside it — the next trend.

📊 Credibility Assessment: 7/10
Strengths: The Schrage book's publication date (2020-09-01), series, case studies and table of contents were confirmed from publisher and bookseller listings. The match between the Japanese guide and the Korean edition was checked on both listings (same authors, 296 pp.).
Gaps: 🔴 "Effectively one" is not the conclusion of an exhaustive survey. It means searches in English, Korean and Japanese surfaced nothing else; out-of-print titles, regional imprints and ebook-only releases were outside the range checked. The Schrage page count varies by source between 264 and 298. The Korean-to-Japanese correspondence is this analysis's inference from matching authors and page count; the copyright page was not inspected.

2. The adjacent genre is already full — and all of it is critique

Where things stand: Books about the effects of recommendation form a genre of their own. Filter bubbles, black-box scores, search bias, algorithms and culture. This ground is not vacant. But the question these books share is "what has recommendation done?" — not "what is recommendation?"

Ground already taken

• Eli Pariser, The Filter Bubble (2011) — personalisation splits our information worlds (Japanese edition from Hayakawa)
• Cathy O'Neil, Weapons of Math Destruction (2016) — the harms of opaque scoring models
• Safiya Noble, Algorithms of Oppression (2018) — racial and gender bias in search engines
• Hannah Fry, Hello World (2018) — the growing influence of algorithms on human life
• Kyle Chayka, Filterworld (2024) — algorithms flatten culture
• Japan has the same lineage in translation and in original work — 『アルゴリズム・AIを疑う』 (Shueisha) · 『AIから読み解く社会』 (University of Tokyo Press)

The genre's own critics point the way

• Chayka's argument: algorithms have replaced human tastemakers to become the primary lever of a globally uniform culture · the turning point was feeds switching from chronological to algorithmic around 2015 · his antidote is "curation", the reimposition of individual human taste
• 🔴 Yet reviewers push back on that framing — they argue Chayka romanticises a past of "human tastemakers" and erases the race, gender, class and power that also decided what became popular
• One review calls the algorithm-versus-human-tastemaker split a false dichotomy outright
• So the genre's weak point is that it drew the wrong axis. Where, then, should the axis go? That is the question sitting vacant

🔗 Related Links: Filterworld review — The Globe and Mail | Filterworld review — NPR | Ten books on algorithmic society (Japanese)

Outlook: The critique genre is close to saturated, and a new book will struggle to find a seat inside it. But the critique levelled at that genre shows a route. Change the axis from "algorithm or human" to "which kind of recommendation" and the same phenomena can be treated from a different position.

📊 Credibility Assessment: 6/10
Strengths: Chayka's argument (replacement of human tastemakers, the 2015 feed switch, curation as antidote) and the reviewers' objections (romanticised past, missing power axis, false dichotomy) were confirmed directly in several reviews. Fry's Hello World (2018) was also confirmed.
Gaps: 🔴 The publication years and arguments of Pariser, O'Neil and Noble were not confirmed in this survey and rest on common knowledge. No book here was read to verify that it "does not ask the definitional question"; the judgement was made from descriptions and reviews. "Close to saturated" rests on a count of titles, not sales data, and is this analysis's impression.

3. 🔴 The closest book comes out in Japan in two weeks

Where things stand: The most unexpected result of this survey. The book that comes closest to asking what recommendation is has not been published yet — it is published in two weeks. Its title is itself a question.

Ryoma Sato, 『推薦システム』 (Recommender Systems)

• Subtitle: "Why are the products a recommender system recommends attractive — or why are they not?"
• Kodansha Scientific · due 10 November 2026 (Tuesday)
• Scope: the basic mechanism → content filtering → collaborative filtering → evaluation methods → recommendation using large language models → social dimensions
• A follow-up to the same author's 『検索システム』 (Search Systems), which carries a question-shaped subtitle in the same mould — "why can a search system find the information you want, or why can it not?"
• The same author's public talk is titled "Recommender systems that do not maximise click-through rate"

It still occupies a different seat

• This is a technical book with Python code. That is a different layer from an Essential Knowledge primer written for readers outside the field
• And the question differs. "Why is it attractive" is a question about quality; "how many kinds are there" is a question about type. The first can be answered end to end while still assuming recommendation is one thing
• ⚠️ Because it is unpublished, the contents, page count and series were not confirmed. It is the first book to check against once it appears
• Note what the talk title shows: criticism of engagement metrics is already inside Japanese technical discourse — it is not the exclusive property of the critique genre

🔗 Related Links: 推薦システム — Kodansha | 検索システム — Kodansha | Recommender systems that do not maximise CTR
Read alongside — the fact that publication is two weeks away is itself a market signal. It means demand for a book that asks about recommendation conceptually is forming now, and at the same time it is evidence that the seat was still empty. The same author put the same question to search two years earlier in 『検索システム』, and the publisher describes that book as having been very well received. In other words, opening a technical subject with a conceptual question worked in Japan, and recommendation is next in line.

Outlook: After 10 November the coordinates of this field shift by one square. But the square that moves is "a technical book that asks a concept", and "a trade book that rewrites the definition" will most likely remain vacant.

📊 Credibility Assessment: 6/10
Strengths: The full title, subtitle, author, publisher and scope were confirmed on the publisher's product page. The release date "10 November (Tue)" comes from the publisher's official account, and 10 November 2026 really is a Tuesday, which cross-checks the year (10 November 2025 was a Monday).
Gaps: 🔴 The book is unpublished, so it could not be read. The contents, page count, series and price were not confirmed (the publisher's page blocks automated access), so it cannot be said that it carries no typology. "A technical book with code" rests on the description's mention of accompanying Python code, and "a question about quality, not type" is this analysis's reading of the title.

4. 🔴 One author wrote "receiving is becoming" and "recommendation" in two separate books

Where things stand: The most important finding of this survey. The insight that receiving a recommendation changes the person who receives it is already in print. So is the history and mechanics of recommendation engines. And yet those two books were never joined — by the same author.

Same author, two books, eight years apart

• 17 July 2012 — Michael Schrage, Who Do You Want Your Customers to Become? (Harvard Business Review Press, 76 pp. ebook)
• Core argument: asking customers to do something different does not go far enough; you must ask them to become something different
• And to enable that change, the firm must invest in the customer's capabilities and competencies — "innovation is an investment in your client, not just a transaction with them"
• 1 September 2020 — the same author, Recommendation Engines (MIT Press)
• 🔴 Yet nowhere in the 2020 table of contents does that 2012 question appear as an axis. The contents run history → mechanics → experience → innovators → future: the grammar of a technical history

Why they never joined — the grammars differ

• "Making someone become" was written in the grammar of corporate strategy and marketing. The subject is the firm, the object is the customer, the purpose is a better customer
• "Recommendation" was written in the grammar of technical history. The subject is the system, the object is the item, the purpose is to predict correctly
• The axis that would join the two grammars — "does holding the item change the person who received it?" — was not found as a classification criterion for recommendation in any book
• 🔴 The academic side is empty too. No literature was found using "state-dependent recommendation" as a taxonomic term, and no philosophical work was found addressing "what is a recommendation" head on — what exists is the lexical and rhetorical distinction between advice and recommendation (a line traced back to Aristotle's Rhetoric)

🔗 Related Links: Who Do You Want Your Customers to Become? — HBR Store | A Challenging Question for Innovators — MIT SMR | Panorama of Recommender Systems to Support Learning

Outlook: The education research literature has known about this split for a long time. Drachsler and colleagues wrote as early as 2009 that e-commerce recommendation relies entirely on consumer interest and taste, whereas what a learner prefers may not be what is pedagogically most appropriate; and the learning-recommendation survey chapter in the 2015 Recommender Systems Handbook (pp. 421–451) sorted the field's systems into seven clusters. But that classification stayed inside academic volumes and never came out as a trade book.

📊 Credibility Assessment: 6/10
Strengths: The 2012 book's publication date (2012-07-17), length (76 pp.) and core argument (not "do something different" but "become something different"; invest in the customer's capabilities) were confirmed from the publisher's store and an MIT SMR article. The 2020 book's table of contents was confirmed from bookseller listings. Common authorship was checked on both.
Gaps: 🔴 "The two books were never joined" rests on the contents and descriptions, not on reading the 2020 book's text. It may address the question somewhere inside. "No taxonomic term found" and "no philosophical work found" are results of public search and are not proof of absence. The Drachsler 2009 quotation is a second-hand citation carried by later literature; the original was not consulted.

5. The nonfiction market now — volume is abundant, trust is scarce

Where things stand: If a book is to be published, under what conditions is it published now? These conditions reshape a manuscript regardless of its subject. In short: you cannot win on volume, and trust has become the differentiator.

Volume is abundant

• A sample of 14,419 self-published genre novels on Amazon (2023–2026): about 20% were flagged as having substantial AI text, meaning over 25% of the prose
• Yet that 20% took only about 12% of sales and 11% of revenue — they occupy the catalogue without taking the earnings (arXiv preprint, not peer reviewed)
• Reporting on the same study summarises it as the number of selling books rising far faster than revenue, so revenue per book fell
• Adult nonfiction was down 9% in early 2026 against the prior year (a Publishers Weekly figure, cited by Ferriss)

So trust acquired a price

• Steven Rosenbaum's 2026 book on AI and truth, The Future of Truth (Matt Holt Books), was found to contain misattributed quotes hallucinated by AI tools
• Karen Hao's Empire of AI, by contrast, won the 2025 National Book Critics Circle Award for Nonfiction — a work built on reporting
• 🔴 The implication is plain. Books that can be written without sources are already abundant, and books that have sources have become the scarce ones. A manuscript carrying primary material and direct measurement is, paradoxically, favoured
• Tim Ferriss reports his own print sales down 46% in 2025 and 57% in 2026 and argues prescriptive nonfiction is a leading indicator (a single author's data)

🔗 Related Links: Generative AI floods and dilutes the market for books (arXiv) | AI slop books flood marketplaces — Fortune | Predictions for the book trade in 2026 — The Bookseller

Outlook: These conditions work in favour of a book that asks a definitional question. Asking what something is cannot be replaced by a summary, and sorting things into kinds cannot be replaced by a search. Conversely, a book that tidies up and introduces an existing debate now sits in the worst seat.

📊 Credibility Assessment: 7/10
Strengths: The sample size (14,419), the flagged share (about 20%) and the sales and revenue shares (about 12% and 11%) come from the arXiv paper; the 9% nonfiction decline and Ferriss's own figures from his post; the award and the misattribution case from their respective entries.
Gaps: 🔴 The arXiv paper has not been peer reviewed (the authors say so themselves). The sample is self-published genre fiction, so it does not transfer directly to trade nonfiction. Ferriss's figures are one author's data, and the 9% decline is a second-hand citation; the Publishers Weekly original was not consulted. "Trust has become the differentiator" is this analysis's reading drawn from one award and one incident.

Comparing the character of the main books

This analysis's assessment on five axes. These are not scores any source published, and Sato (2026) is unpublished, so its values are inferred from the title and description.

Five market shifts

  • There is one trade book on recommendation: apart from Schrage's Recommendation Engines (2020), no dedicated recommendation book for non-specialist readers was found. The rest are textbooks and guides.
  • The critique genre is close to saturated: five books from The Filter Bubble to Filterworld treat what recommendation has done. What it is is not among them.
  • Demand for a conceptual book is forming now: the nearest title comes out in Japan on 10 November 2026, after the same author's search volume was well received first.
  • The core insights are already scattered: "make them become" sits in a management book, "recommendation" in a technical one, "learner preference ≠ appropriateness" in an academic chapter. Nothing was found binding them on one axis.
  • Trust has become the differentiator: 20% of a self-published catalogue was AI-written and nonfiction fell 9% year on year. A sourced manuscript is, paradoxically, scarce.

Application Ideas

Approaches observed in the market.

1. Check unpublished titles first - a book two weeks out may sit closer than anything already on the shelf

2. Declare the layer up front - critique, concept or textbook decides the reader and the review route

3. Put primary measurement in the text - material that a summary cannot replace is what differentiates in this market

Executive Summary

Key points

  • The book market splits into two clusters: five books criticising what recommendation has done to society, and six textbooks and guides teaching how to build one. The layer between them, asking what recommendation is, holds a single volume.
  • No book was found sorting recommendation into kinds: the academic side showed no literature using "state-dependent recommendation" as a taxonomic term, and the seven-cluster classification of educational recommenders stayed inside an academic chapter.
  • The core insights sit in two books by one author: "you must ask customers to become something different" (2012) and "the history and mechanics of recommendation engines" (2020) were written by the same person and never joined on one axis.
  • The closest competing title comes out in two weeks: Ryoma Sato's 『推薦システム』 (Kodansha, due 2026-11-10) poses a conceptual question in its very title. But it is a technical book with code, and it asks about quality rather than type.
  • Market conditions favour a sourced manuscript: 20% of a self-published catalogue was AI-written yet took only 12% of sales. Adult nonfiction fell 9% year on year, and the same year brought both a hallucinated-citation incident and an award for reported work.

Reference points by situation

Overall assessment: Neutral. A confirmed gap is an opportunity, but the possibility that the gap exists because there is no demand cannot be ruled out. A book that asks a definitional question has no evidence it will sell like a critique, and no readership as clear as a textbook's. That said, a volume asking the same question of search appeared first, was well received, and its sequel arrives in two weeks — which reads as a signal that this seat is empty but not dead.

Key Terminology

Collaborative filtering — filtering by gathering what other people did rather than by analysing content. Like footprints across fresh snow becoming the path everyone follows.
Implicit feedback — not ratings a user deliberately left, but the behavioural record (clicks, watch time, purchases) that accumulates by itself while the service is used.
Filter bubble — personalisation taken far enough that each person ends up enclosed in a different information world. The term spread through the 2011 book of that name.
Performative prediction — a situation where issuing the prediction changes the very world being predicted. A navigation app announcing "this side street is faster" sends everyone down it and jams it.
Algorithmic confounding — when a recommender is retrained on the choice records it produced, the data ends up recording the recommender's past decisions rather than the users' taste.
Essential Knowledge series — MIT Press's line of short concept primers on single subjects, written for readers outside the field.
Self-publishing — releasing a book directly to retailers without a publisher. With no gatekeeping step, catalogues grow quickly.
