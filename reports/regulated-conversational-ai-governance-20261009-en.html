Design From the Proof
— How Finance, Law and Government Build Conversational AI

Ordinary chatbots start with features; regulated industries start with what must survive an audit
Report date: October 9, 2026 | Standard report (5-8 pages) | Data collected: October 9, 2026

Top 5 Trends

The central claim of this report
Ordinary chatbots are built first and validated later. In regulated industries it runs the other way. What must be proven (governance) determines how the system is built (design) and in what order it is built (process). Rather than listing regulations, this report translates each into the work it creates on the development floor.

1. Why it has three names — academia, industry and regulators see different things

Situation: Systems that handle conversations with something to finish — a booking, an enquiry, a filing — get different names depending on who is speaking. They point at the same thing with different emphasis, which means the name you search by determines the literature you find.

Three naming traditions

• Academia: Task-Oriented Dialogue (TOD), built from four parts — natural language understanding, state tracking, policy, and generation
• Industry: Conversational AI or virtual agent. Add tool calling and retrieval and it becomes an LLM agent
• Regulators: high-risk AI system. The name hardened once the EU AI Act classified credit scoring, essential public services and administration of justice as high-risk

Domain names and the shared shape

• Finance: conversational banking, robo-advisory
• Law: legal intake and triage
• Public sector: civic service chatbot, e-government assistant
All three share the same shape — advising, guiding, intake and triage — and all three must include a human handoff that passes the final decision to a person

🔗 Related Links: EU AI Act portal | ACL Anthology

Outlook: The split in naming is not merely terminological. Academia looks at structure, industry at product, regulators at risk. Building in a regulated industry means reading all three — and it is the regulatory view that sets the build order.

📊 Credibility Assessment: 7/10
Strengths: The four-part TOD architecture and the EU AI Act's high-risk classification are confirmed in academic literature and in the legislation respectively.
Weaknesses: The "three naming traditions" framing is this report's own organisation, not an accepted taxonomy. In practice the boundaries blur.

2. When liability became real — a ruling, an enforcement action, a reversal

Situation: "If it is wrong, liability follows" is no longer a warning. Between 2024 and 2025 it became fact in four distinct ways. What matters is that each case points at a different place where the defect sat.

① The ruling — what the chatbot says, the company said

Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024 BCCRT 149)
In November 2022, after losing a grandparent, Jake Moffatt was told by Air Canada's chatbot that he could claim a bereavement fare within 90 days of travel. The actual policy accepted no claims after travel.
The airline argued the chatbot was "a separate legal entity that is responsible for its own actions." Tribunal member Christopher Rivers called that submission "remarkable" and rejected it. The chatbot forms part of Air Canada's website, so Air Canada is fully responsible for what it says. The tribunal ordered the fare difference plus interest and fees.

② The enforcement — not testing was the violation

DoNotPay — US Federal Trade Commission
The FTC filed a complaint in September 2024 against the subscription service marketed as "the world's first robot lawyer." The Commission voted 5-0 on January 16, 2025 to approve the final order, which required $193,000 in monetary relief and notice to subscribers from 2021 to 2023.
Note what the violation was. The FTC found the company had not tested whether its "AI lawyer" performed at the level of a human lawyer, and had not hired or retained attorneys to test the quality and accuracy of its law-related features. The defect was not in an answer. It was the absence of a validation process.

③ The reversal — when cost dominates, quality drops

Klarna
In February 2024 the company announced its AI assistant had handled 2.3 million chats in 30 days — the workload of 700 full-time agents — automating 67% of conversations with roughly $40 million in projected annual savings.
Then in May 2025 it reversed course and began rehiring human agents, after customers complained about generic answers and the system's inability to handle complicated, nuanced cases.
CEO Sebastian Siemiatkowski told Bloomberg: "As cost unfortunately seems to have been a too predominant evaluation factor when organizing this, what you end up having is lower quality."

④ Public sector — a disclaimer does not fix it

New York City's MyCity chatbot
In 2024 the city's business-support chatbot gave unlawful advice: that employers could take a cut of workers' tips, that no rules required notice of schedule changes, and that landlords could refuse tenants on rental assistance — the last of which has been illegal since 2008.
Rather than taking it offline, the city updated its disclaimers: responses "may sometimes be inaccurate or incomplete," and businesses should "not use its responses as legal or professional advice." Mayor Eric Adams acknowledged "it's wrong in some areas, and we've got to fix it," while stressing it was a pilot.

🔗 Related Links: FTC — DoNotPay final order | Klarna | Air Canada | New York City

Outlook: The four cases point in four different directions. The ruling settles where liability sits; the enforcement action exposes a missing validation process; Klarna exposes how evaluation metrics were designed; New York shows the limits of a disclaimer. The three sections that follow are the answers to those four.

📊 Credibility Assessment: 9/10
Strengths: All four are confirmed in primary sources — the case citation (2024 BCCRT 149) and the tribunal member's words, the FTC's own press release with the vote and the amount, Klarna's CEO quoted directly, and the mayor's statement plus the city's response as reported.
Weaknesses: The Air Canada award is confirmed only as "fare difference plus interest and fees," so no total figure is stated.

3. The governance layer — what must be proven

Situation: Regulation is not a list of prohibitions. It is a list of things to prove. It asks less what you must not do than how you will show what you did. That is why the development method unwinds backwards from here.

United States and EU

• NIST AI RMF 1.0 — four functions, GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE and MANAGE, structuring risk across the system lifecycle
• NIST AI 600-1 (Generative AI Profile) — organises risks unique to or worsened by generative AI into 12 categories, including confabulation, harmful content, privacy and security vulnerabilities
• EU AI Act — obliges high-risk systems to maintain risk management, data governance, record-keeping, human oversight and accuracy. Article 50 transparency duties applied from August 2, 2026, with fines up to €15 million or 3% of worldwide turnover
• ISO/IEC 42001 — the AI management system certification standard

Korea and Japan

• Korea's AI Basic Act took effect on January 22, 2026. It makes Korea the second jurisdiction after the EU, and the first in Asia-Pacific, to have a comprehensive binding national AI law
• High-impact AI carries five core obligations applying to developers and deploying operators alike: a risk management plan, explanations, user protection measures, human supervision, and documentation
• AI-based products and services require advance notice to individuals, and a Korea-based local representative must be designated
• Fines top out at 30 million won, with a guidance period of at least one year
• Japan's AI Business Operator Guidelines v1.1 (March 2025) consolidated three earlier guidelines from the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications and METI

Finance carries one additional layer: model risk management (MRM). An LLM is a "model," so validation, monitoring and revalidation cycles must exist on paper. Not a sense that conversation quality is good, but a record of when, by whom, and how it was validated.

🔗 Related Links: NIST AI RMF | ISO/IEC 42001 | EU AI Act | Japan MIC | US Federal Reserve SR 26-2

Outlook: Four jurisdictions write different laws but ask for similar things. Human oversight, explanation and record-keeping appear in all of them. Those three determine the design principles in the next section outright.

📊 Credibility Assessment: 8/10
Strengths: Korea's effective date (January 22, 2026), its five obligations and fine ceiling, Japan's v1.1 guidelines (March 2025) and their consolidation, and NIST's four functions and 12 risk categories are all sourced.
Weaknesses: The specifics of applying financial MRM to LLMs — revalidation cycles in particular — were not confirmed explicitly in published supervisory guidance, so only the general principle is described.

4. The design layer — code decides, the model writes

Situation: Translate "human oversight, explanation and record-keeping" into code and one design principle remains. Deterministic code makes the decisions; the LLM turns fixed values into sentences. In a regulated industry this separation is not taste but an audit requirement, because asking the LLM afterwards why it answered that way is not an explanation.

Four design rules

• Separate decision from phrasing: whether to ask, answer, refuse or hand off is decided by code
• Keep state outside the context window: slots, sources, confirmations, refusal history and evidence are preserved as variables. Kept only in conversation history, they cannot be reproduced
• Retrieval with forced citation: every factual sentence is bound to retrieved source text; with no source, the system says there is none
• Guardrails on both sides: input (PII, jailbreak, out of scope) and output (prohibited phrasing, protected attributes, categorical legal determinations) each pass through code filters

How regulated industries pick a platform

Not by a feature list, but by three questions.
• Where the data sits: is on-premises or a dedicated environment possible (Rasa, Cognigy, boost.ai, Kore.ai)
• Audit logs: can state and decisions be recorded for every turn
• Model pinning: can a model version be fixed. If the model changes underneath, prior validation is void
Hyperscalers (Google Dialogflow CX, Microsoft Copilot Studio, Amazon Lex, IBM watsonx Assistant) bundle operational convenience with compliance features, but the data-location question must be satisfied before they enter the shortlist

This report views the same requirement from the system and the development process rather than the model. Whether a model respects its scope, and whether a system enforces that scope in code, are a matched pair of questions.

Outlook: Human handoff is not a safety net bolted on afterwards but a feature designed from the start. Without defined conditions for handing off and a defined way of carrying conversation state across, a system under failure emits an empty answer. In a regulated industry an empty answer is worse than a handoff.

📊 Credibility Assessment: 7/10
Strengths: That human oversight, explanation and record-keeping are explicit regulatory requirements was established in section 3, and the design principles follow logically. The platform list is confirmed in each vendor's public material.
Weaknesses: The formulation "code decides, the model writes" and the three platform criteria are this report's recommendations, not language taken from any supervisory guidance.

5. The process layer — build the ruler before the feature

Situation: In regulated industries the build order differs. It is evaluation-driven development. Rather than building features and measuring afterwards, the measurement tools and the question set come first, and features are laid on top. Recall that the DoNotPay violation was precisely the absence of this process, and the reason becomes obvious.

Two axes plus a standing condition

• Task success: destination reached, turn budget (how many turns it took)
• Dialogue cost: re-asks, handoff rate, response time
This two-axis framing descends from the dialogue evaluation framework known as PARADISE
• Safety sits above as a standing condition: discrimination, data leakage and out-of-scope answers are treated as pass or fail, not as a score

Operating discipline

• Release gate: on a version bump, run the full fixed question set; fail if it regresses against the baseline
• Red teaming: dedicated question sets for jailbreaks, PII elicitation and protected-attribute discrimination. External red teams are customary in finance and law
• Transcript review: some defects appear only when a person reads real conversation logs, even when every automated metric passes
• Keep units small: one task = one metric = one place to change. Large refactors cost too much to revalidate
• Reproducibility: pin model, prompt and code versions together

There is a published example of this process in practice: the UK government's GOV.UK Chat. Across two public pilots over 18 months, more than 10,000 participants asked around 26,000 questions, and accuracy rose from an early benchmark of 76% to 90%. 73% said they found it useful and 64% said they were satisfied.

What deserves attention is not the number but how it was measured. Answers were assessed using a combination of subject-matter experts and evaluation tools, and assessors rated an answer "accurate" only if it met all the standards of published GOV.UK content. The definition of accurate came first, and because the same definition was applied repeatedly, the path from 76 to 90 became visible.

The tooling ecosystem divides along the same process. NeMo Guardrails (Apache 2.0) defines conversation flows themselves through a domain-specific language called Colang, with five rail types — input, dialog, retrieval, execution and output — so that an assistant is held to approved subjects rather than merely having isolated sentences filtered. Patronus AI's Lynx uses chain-of-thought reasoning, aimed at regulated industries that need documented hallucination detection with explainable outputs. Giskard is building open-source evaluation tooling covering hallucination, toxic content and security vulnerabilities together.

Outlook: The order is hardening: build the evaluation system before choosing the platform. Klarna's lesson lands here. When cost becomes the predominant evaluation factor, quality falls. What you measure decides what you build.

📊 Credibility Assessment: 8/10
Strengths: GOV.UK Chat's accuracy trajectory (76→90%), participation scale, satisfaction figures and — critically — its assessment method are confirmed in UK Government Digital Service material. NeMo Guardrails' five rails and Colang, and Patronus's regulated-industry orientation, are confirmed in vendor documentation.
Weaknesses: The original PARADISE paper's year and authors were not re-confirmed against primary sources in this round, so they are not stated. "Evaluation-driven development" is not a formally standardised industry term.

Five market shifts

  • What the chatbot says is now what the company said: the "separate legal entity" defence was rejected in a tribunal.
  • Not testing became the violation: the issue was the missing validation process, not the quality of an answer.
  • Cost-led evaluation degraded quality: Klarna was rehiring human agents within roughly a year.
  • Disclaimers do not cover public-sector error: the New York case demonstrates it.
  • Regulation is converging: the EU, Korea, Japan and the US all require human oversight, explanation and records.

Application Ideas

Approaches observed in the market.

1. Locate your tier - Write down where each running or planned conversational service sits across tiers 1-4, and check the proof that tier requires against what exists

2. Define accurate first - Before adding features, document what counts as accurate, build a fixed question set, and measure a baseline

3. Test the handoff path - Verify in practice whether a failure, quota breach or out-of-scope question produces an empty answer or reaches a person

Executive Summary

Key Findings

  • Liability was settled by ruling: Air Canada argued its chatbot was a separate legal entity; the tribunal rejected that and held the company fully responsible.
  • The violation was process, not answers: the FTC found DoNotPay had not retained attorneys to test quality and accuracy, and a $193,000 order was finalised in January 2025.
  • Cost-led evaluation degrades quality: Klarna announced automation equal to 700 agents, then resumed hiring human agents roughly a year later.
  • Regulation asks for proof, not prohibition: the EU, Korea, Japan and the US commonly require human oversight, explanation and records. Korea's AI Basic Act took effect on January 22, 2026.
  • A published model exists: GOV.UK Chat defined "accurate" first and measured repeatedly, raising it from 76% to 90%.

Guidance by situation

Already in operation → start by locating your tier and testing the handoff
Why: knowing which tier you are running at, and whether a failure emits an empty answer, is the cheapest check available.
About to build → start with the evaluation system
Why: build the measurement after the features and there is no baseline, so improvement cannot be demonstrated.
Facing imminent compliance → start with records and human oversight
Why: all four jurisdictions require them, and they are the kind of evidence that cannot be created retroactively.

Overall sentiment: Neutral to positive. Rulings and enforcement are a near-term burden, but having what must be proven stated concretely makes design clearer. Tier 4 decision support raises requirements sharply, so for now building the proof system at tiers 1–2 is the realistic target.

Key Terminology

Task-Oriented Dialogue (TOD) — A system handling conversations with something to finish, such as a booking, an enquiry or a filing. Unlike a chit-chat bot, it has a destination.
Human handoff — Passing a conversation to a human agent when the system cannot, or must not, handle it. In regulated industries it is part of the product.
Provenance — Recording, at the moment of answering, which clause, term or notice the answer came from. Later explanations draw only on that record.
Guardrail — A mechanism inspecting inputs and outputs to block risky content. Not a request made in a prompt, but a filter enforced in code.
Confabulation — A model producing plausible-sounding content that is not true. NIST classifies it among the risks unique to generative AI.
Model risk management (MRM) — The financial sector's documented system for validating, monitoring and revalidating models. An LLM is a model and falls within scope.
High-impact / high-risk AI — AI in domains that can materially affect life, safety or fundamental rights. Korea's AI Basic Act calls it "high-impact," the EU AI Act "high-risk," each attaching additional obligations.
