Why Rival Standards
End Up Under the Same Roof

What foundations take on, and the strengths and limits of that structure
Report date: October 9, 2026 | Standard report (5-8 pages) | Data collected: October 9, 2026

Top 5 Trends

The central claim of this report
For a technology built by one company to become an industry standard, it has to leave that company's hands. A foundation is the neutral ground that receives the transfer, and in AI those transfers are happening all at once. Rather than listing foundations, this report covers why companies give away their own assets and what that structure guarantees — and what it does not.

1. Three rivals founded one foundation together — the Agentic AI Foundation

Situation: In December 2025 the Linux Foundation announced the formation of the Agentic AI Foundation (AAIF). What matters is how it was formed. This was not an empty shell that later gathered projects: three companies each brought a core asset, and the foundation was built out of them.

Founding project contributions

• MCP — Anthropic's Model Context Protocol
• goose — Block's agent tooling
• AGENTS.md — donated by OpenAI
These three compete in the market. That they stood together as founding members under one name is the substance of this event.

Who gathered

• Founding: OpenAI · Anthropic · Block
• Backing: Google · Microsoft · AWS · Bloomberg · Cloudflare
• Structure: AAIF operates as a directed fund under the Linux Foundation
• Since: Google's A2A joined as a hosted project on August 17, 2026

Add the move from a year earlier and the picture completes. The AGNTCY collective — Cisco, LangChain, LlamaIndex, Dell, Oracle and Red Hat — donated ACP to the Linux Foundation in July 2025. Three of the major agent-era specifications moved under the same umbrella within roughly a year.

🔗 Related Links: AAIF formation announcement (Linux Foundation) | Anthropic — donating MCP | OpenAI — donating AGENTS.md

Outlook: When a standards contest moves inside a foundation, its character changes. It stops being a fight to push rivals out of the market and becomes a contest over board seats and decision procedures at the same table.

📊 Credibility Assessment: 9/10
Strengths: The Linux Foundation press release, Anthropic's and OpenAI's own announcements, and TechCrunch's coverage (December 9, 2025) all agree. Founding projects and participants are confirmed in primary sources.
Weaknesses: How AAIF's directed-fund structure differs in governance from an ordinary Linux Foundation project was not confirmed in detail from public material.

2. What a foundation provides — neutrality, trademark, legal, operations

Situation: What a company receives in exchange for handing over a project is more concrete than it sounds. Foundations exist to formally govern open source projects and ensure no single vendor holds too much control. That role is carried out through practical work, not declarations.

What the foundation supplies

• Legal and licensing support: license hygiene, contributor agreements
• Trademark management: who may use the name and logo, and how
• Project operations tooling: established collaboration infrastructure and process
• Event support: the venues that gather a community
• Neutrality as a signal: "this is not one company's" is itself an adoption argument

What is given up

In donating the software, a company relinquishes control of it.
• Other foundation members are granted the same rights
• Those members include competitors
• Direction can no longer be set alone; process must be followed
This is the essential price of donation.

Continue reading — This site's October 9, 2026 report, "There Was No Protocol War — How MCP and A2A Divided the Layers", argued that the two specifications ended not in competition but in a division of layers. The institutional device that made that ending possible is precisely this foundation structure. Neither side needed to beat the other once neither belonged to any one company.

Outlook: Neutrality is not free. It is traded for control, and the trade works because a standard everyone uses is larger than a standard you own alone.

📊 Credibility Assessment: 8/10
Strengths: The legal, trademark and operational support foundations provide, and the price of "relinquishing control and granting competitors the same rights," are confirmed in open source governance research.
Weaknesses: No quantitative material was obtained showing which of these supports is decisive in practice.

3. Different types decide different things — a map in five branches

Situation: "Foundation" and "standards body" are often used interchangeably, but they do different work. Knowing which is which makes it clear which question belongs to whom.

The formally recognised side

• ISO · IEC · ITU — internationally recognised standards bodies. They produce certifiable specifications such as ISO/IEC 42001
• What they issue are de jure standards
• Certificates can be granted, and regulation can reference them

Not formally recognised, but widely used

• W3C · IETF · IEEE · OASIS · OMG
• Strictly speaking the W3C is not a standards body. Groups of experts produce Recommendations, with no power to enforce implementation — yet most are taken as de facto standards
• IETF defines Internet standards through RFCs; IEEE issues LAN standards such as Ethernet

Add two open source branches and the domain consortia, and you have five. Governance foundations such as Linux Foundation, Apache and Eclipse; the domain foundations beneath them such as CNCF, Agentic AI Foundation, PyTorch Foundation, LF AI & Data and OpenSSF; and domain consortia such as 1EdTech (Open Badges, CLR), HL7 (health) and OGC (geospatial).

The practical value of the distinction is clear. Foundations run code and communities; standards bodies confer official status on specifications. There is also a path from de facto to de jure — PDF went to ISO, HTML to ISO/IEC.

🔗 Related Links: Linux Foundation | Apache Software Foundation | CNCF | W3C | IETF | 1EdTech

Outlook: Once AI specifications settle inside foundations, the likely next step is movement toward formally recognised bodies, because a form that regulation can reference becomes necessary.

📊 Credibility Assessment: 8/10
Strengths: The recognised status of ISO, IEC and ITU, the W3C's position as a body issuing Recommendations, and the de facto to de jure examples (PDF, HTML) are confirmed in standardisation literature.
Weaknesses: The five-branch division is this report's own organisation. It is not an official taxonomy, and some organizations straddle boundaries.

4. Why companies give it away — what is lost, what is gained

Situation: Donation is not altruism. It is a calculated trade. MCP illustrates the calculation well. After Anthropic let go of control, MCP became a specification with over 400 million monthly SDK downloads, deployed by 28% of the Fortune 500. Those numbers were out of reach for one company's connection format.

What is gained

• The standing of neutrality: competitors become able to adopt it. This is the largest gain
• Adoption speed: "it belongs to no one" makes approval easier
• Distributed legal burden: the foundation takes on trademark and licensing
• Longevity: the specification survives even if the originating company's strategy changes

What is lost

• Directional control: decisions follow process rather than one company
• Speed: consensus is slower
• Differentiation: competitors use the same specification
Companies still donate, for a clear reason: control over a technology that never becomes a standard is worth little.

The same calculation explains three rivals founding AAIF together. A fragmented agent ecosystem costs all of them. Competing on products atop a shared specification is worth more to all three than each holding its own.

🔗 Related Links: TechCrunch coverage | AGNTCY

Outlook: The calculation holds while the market is still unsettled. Once a winner is established the incentive to donate disappears. That transfers clustered now is itself a signal that the agent market is still forming.

📊 Credibility Assessment: 7/10
Strengths: The gain/loss structure of donation (neutrality gained, control lost) is confirmed in open source governance research, and MCP's adoption figures are corroborated across sources.
Weaknesses: The reading of why three rivals founded it together is this report's inference. Each company's official explanation stays at the general level of neutral development and standardisation.

5. Being in a foundation does not make it safe — the limits of the structure

Situation: The label "foundation project" signals neutrality, but the character of that neutrality differs by foundation. The sharpest contrast is between the Linux Foundation and the Apache Software Foundation. Both are widely trusted, yet who decides is the exact opposite.

Linux Foundation — corporate membership

• Legal form: 501(c)(6) trade organization
• Members: corporate, with tiered sponsorship
• Fees: Silver $5,000–$20,000 (by employee count) · Gold $100,000 · Platinum $500,000
• Platinum includes a dedicated board seat
• Large sponsors hold structured governance seats and help direct projects

Apache Software Foundation — individual merit

• Legal form: 501(c)(3) charity
• Members: individuals, earned exclusively through sustained contribution merit
• Pay-to-play governance is strictly prohibited — a company can donate millions and receive zero influence over technical direction; only individual code contributors earn votes
• Operations: roughly $2.29M a year in corporate sponsorship, with decentralised project management committees

What to read here is not superiority but a difference in design. Corporate membership is strong at gathering resources and building industry consensus quickly; individual merit is more resistant to the influence of any single company. Knowing which foundation a project sits in tells you whose hands its direction is in.

Three further limits deserve attention. Decision speed slows under consensus structures; the possibility of a fork always remains under an open license; and above all, "being in a foundation" guarantees neither code quality nor security. A foundation provides governance; it does not perform your audit.

🔗 Related Links: Apache Software Foundation | Linux Foundation | OpenSSF

Outlook: As AI specifications gather into foundations, "which foundation" becomes the next question. A specification under corporate membership is necessarily more sensitive to the interests of its largest sponsors, and knowing that sensitivity belongs in an adopting organization's risk management.

📊 Credibility Assessment: 8/10
Strengths: Fee tiers, the 501(c)(6) versus 501(c)(3) distinction, and Apache's prohibition on pay-to-play alongside its individual merit model are confirmed in each foundation's public material and governance analyses.
Weaknesses: No case study showing how far large sponsors have actually changed technical decisions was obtained in this round. This describes a structural possibility, not an accusation about a specific case.

Five market shifts

  • Rivals founded a foundation together: OpenAI, Anthropic and Block each brought an asset to establish AAIF.
  • Transfers clustered within a year: ACP (July 2025), MCP (December 2025) and A2A (August 2026) all moved under the same umbrella.

Application Ideas

Approaches observed in the market.

1. Check where your dependencies sit - Identify which foundation each specification you rely on belongs to, and whether that foundation runs on corporate membership or individual merit

2. Trace the transfer date - Knowing when a specification moved from a company to a foundation lets you read how its direction changed around that point

3. Check the path to formal recognition - Where regulatory compliance matters, confirm in advance whether a specification has a route toward a body such as ISO

Executive Summary

Key Findings

  • Three rivals founded a foundation together: in December 2025 OpenAI, Anthropic and Block established the Agentic AI Foundation with AGENTS.md, MCP and goose.
  • Transfers clustered within a year: ACP (July 2025), MCP (December 2025) and A2A (August 2026) all moved into the Linux Foundation family.
  • The trade is explicit: neutrality and adoption speed are gained; directional control and differentiation are given up.
  • Decision rights differ by foundation: the Linux Foundation runs corporate membership (Platinum at $500,000 includes a board seat); Apache runs individual merit and forbids pay-to-play.
  • Membership does not guarantee quality: a foundation supplies governance, not code audit or security.

Guidance by situation

Choosing a specification → start with the foundation's membership structure
Why: who holds the seats is the best available predictor of where that specification goes next.
Facing regulatory requirements → start with the path to formal recognition
Why: foundation specifications cannot issue certificates. Whether a route to a body such as ISO exists matters.
Wanting your own technology to become a standard → act before the market settles
Why: the incentive to donate only holds while a market is forming. After a winner emerges the trade no longer works.

Overall sentiment: Positive. Specifications converging on neutral ground rather than being tied to one company favours adopters. It does need to be paired with the caution of not reading "it is in a foundation" as a warranty of quality or neutrality.

Key Terminology

Foundation — An organization created to run an open source project in a state where it belongs to no single company, taking on legal, trademark and operational work.
Directed fund — A sub-organization created inside a parent foundation for a specific purpose. The Agentic AI Foundation is a directed fund within the Linux Foundation.
De jure standard — A standard issued by an officially recognised body such as ISO. Certificates can be granted and regulation can reference it.
De facto standard — Something widely used enough to function as a standard without official approval. PDF and HTML later became formal standards.
501(c)(6) · 501(c)(3) — US non-profit classifications. (c)(6) is a trade organization and (c)(3) a charity; they differ in membership structure and tax treatment.
Pay-to-play — A structure where money buys influence over decisions. Apache forbids it; the Linux Foundation ties board seats to sponsorship tiers.
Fork — Copying an open source project and developing it in a separate direction. Always possible under an open license, it is the ultimate check on governance.
