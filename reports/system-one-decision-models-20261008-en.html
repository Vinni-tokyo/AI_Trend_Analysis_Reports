Decision Models, Surveyed Globally —
Jev, Laya, and the Three Weeks That Made "System One"

Models that judge instead of write: 25-plus open clones, a wire format standardized by Ollama, and the first independent benchmark — where a 2023 classifier beat Jev
Report date: October 8, 2026 | Comprehensive report | Data collected: 2026-10-08

Top 5 Trends

1. Jev — A Model That Returns a Verdict Instead of Prose

Where things stand: TypeSafe AI released Jev on September 15, 2026, naming the category itself: a "System One model." Where a large language model (LLM) generates text, Jev answers typed questions about a text and attaches probabilities. The point is that software can use those values directly as a branch condition. The same day, TypeSafe came out of stealth with a $40 million seed round led by DCVC; Forbes reported a post-money valuation of roughly $200 million. The founders are Diogo Almeida — a former OpenAI researcher and co-inventor of RLHF and ChatGPT — along with Erik Gafni and Sasha Sheng.

The three question types (primitives)

• Choice: one option out of up to 255, plus a probability distribution across them
• Score: a position on an ordered rubric (2–10 levels), plus a distribution over levels
• Noul: the probability that a condition is true (yes/no)
• All three return a confidence value alongside the answer
• Training method is RLCD (Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions) — post-training aimed at correct verdicts and usable probability estimates rather than preferred conversational replies

Operational specs per the official docs

• Version: jev-1.13.0, served at POST /v1/systemone
• Context: 64k tokens per request; a 32k budget for state plus the longest single question
• Parallel evaluation: ingests state once and evaluates every question against it in parallel
• Price: $0.042 per million input tokens; output tokens are free
• Limits: 100K tokens per second / 80 requests per second
• Constraints: text only (no image or audio), English is the primary language, and no per-account fine-tuning — identical weights across all customers

⚠️ One figure we do not adopt: Sequoia partner Pat Grady said Jev went "from zero to $100 million in revenue in seven days," and several outlets reprinted it. This report does not use that number. The disclosed seed was $40 million at a post-money valuation of roughly $200 million; a seed-stage company booking $100 million in a week is hard to reconcile with that valuation. TypeSafe has named no customers.
📊 Credibility Assessment: 9/10
Strengths: Specs, pricing, limits and constraints come from TypeSafe's own documentation (docs.typesafe.ai/models); the funding facts come from the Business Wire release, as do the founder details.
Weaknesses: Parameter count, architecture and the RLCD recipe are unpublished, so the "why is it fast" claim cannot be verified. The official documentation gives no latency figures at all.
🔗 Related Links: TypeSafe official model docs | $40M seed announcement (Business Wire) | RLCD explained (Turing Post)

Outlook: Jev's real invention is more likely the interface than the model. The contract — three typed question kinds plus probabilities — holds regardless of what sits behind it, and within three weeks more than 25 models had implemented that contract.

2. A Three-Week Clone Rush — Laya and 25-Plus Open-Weight Implementations

Where things stand: Three days after Jev, on September 18, 2026, Convai Innovations published Laya's weights to Hugging Face under Apache 2.0. Laya takes a fundamentally different route. It is not a shrunken LLM but an encoder (BERT family) with a typed decision head — a non-autoregressive model that generates no text and produces its answer in a single forward pass. It ships as ModernBERT-large 421M for English, mmBERT-base 322M for 100-plus languages, and a router that detects the script and dispatches accordingly. Over the following month, open models implementing Jev's three question types arrived in a flood.

Laya's measured specs (HF model card)

• Checkpoints: 421M (English) / 322M (multilingual) / 421M (typed-decisions fine-tune)
• License: Apache 2.0 · Runs on: CPU, CUDA, Apple Silicon
• Latency: about 33ms single-shot, 7.2ms per question when batched
• Cost: $0 per token on your own hardware (infrastructure cost only)
• Context: 512 tokens English / 1,024 multilingual — an order of magnitude below Jev's 64k
• Training scale: fine-tuned on 1,200 cases (6,000 decisions), evaluated on 400 cases (2,000 decisions)

The derivative spectrum (a sample)

• Decider (Mapika): Qwen3.5-based 2B/4B/35B-a3b — the 35B activates 3B, with 8 of 256 routed experts live
• Nimble (Bespoke Labs): 9B fine-tuned from Qwen3.5-9B, 8,192 tokens, 255 choices
• Clef (Cloudflare, Oct 1): 27B/9B Apache 2.0, Workers AI, $0.09 per million tokens
• Anarkali (ToufiqQureshi): 68M — the smallest encoder, claiming to beat Jev at one-sixth the size
• AnyJev (Nokia Applied Research): training-free — reads hidden states from an existing LLM with debiasing
• Jev-Omni: 12B multimodal (text, images, audio, video) — territory Jev does not cover

📊 Credibility Assessment: 8/10
Strengths: Laya's figures were read directly from the official Hugging Face model card (convaiinnovations/laya-typed-decisions) — primary material down to training and evaluation case counts and per-workflow accuracy.
Weaknesses: The list of 25-plus derivatives rests mainly on a single aggregator site; individual repositories were not exhaustively verified. Claims like "beats Jev at one-sixth the size" are all publisher self-assessments.
🔗 Related Links: Laya (Hugging Face) | laya-typed-decisions model card | Decider (GitHub) | Nimble (Ollama)

Outlook: What the clone rush shows is not who won but proof that the barrier to entry is effectively zero. Everything from a 68M encoder to a 35B mixture-of-experts — and even approaches requiring no training at all (AnyJev, SemIf) — satisfied the same contract.

3. The Wire Format Got Standardized — Ollama Shipped /v1/systemone

Where things stand: On September 29, 2026, Ollama 0.35 added a /v1/systemone endpoint. The crucial detail is that it is wire-compatible with Jev. A commercial API's request and response shape became the standard endpoint of a local runtime in two weeks. The same day, OpenAI launched a Decisions API (a GPT-6 Luna variant, 150ms latency, limited preview) and Liquid AI launched d1; two days later Cloudflare published Clef. Three hyperscale platforms entered a category that was two weeks old.

Ollama 0.35 (2026-09-29)

• Endpoint: /v1/systemone — you pass the input text, the question and the choices together
• Response: not prose but typed answers plus probabilities plus usage
• Question types: choice, noul, score (the same names Jev uses)
• Three launch models: Nimble 9B (Bespoke Labs) and Together AI's Tev1 at 4B and 0.8B (experimental)
• Effect: no extra cost and lower latency when run locally — aimed at ticket triage, model routing and content moderation

Three platforms that arrived the same week

• OpenAI Decisions API (Sep 29): GPT-6 Luna variant, 150ms, limited preview, API only
• Liquid AI d1 (Sep 29): free tier, self-reported first place on the Jev Decision Index
• Cloudflare Clef (Oct 1): Qwen fine-tunes at 27B/9B, Apache 2.0, Workers AI plus self-hosting, $0.09 per million tokens
• What it means: the edge (Cloudflare), the frontier (OpenAI) and the local machine (Ollama) opened simultaneously behind one interface

📊 Credibility Assessment: 8.5/10
Strengths: Ollama 0.35's new endpoint and its three launch models were consistent across multiple independent reports, and we confirmed by direct comparison that the path matches POST /v1/systemone in TypeSafe's official documentation.
Weaknesses: OpenAI's Decisions API and Liquid AI's d1 are in limited preview or rest on self-published rankings, so their specs could not be pinned to primary documentation. "First on the Jev Decision Index" is a vendor claim.
🔗 Related Links: Ollama releases | Nimble on Ollama | Cloudflare Workers AI | Liquid AI

Outlook: The category has no winner yet, but the interface looks settled. Once the wire format is standard, models become swappable parts and competition drops from model quality to latency, unit price and where the thing runs. That structure works against Jev.

4. The First Independent Benchmark Pushed Back — A 2023 DeBERTa Classifier Beat Jev

Where things stand: On October 2, 2026, Red Hat's developer blog published a like-for-like comparison of nine models across four paradigms. It used two balanced datasets from the NeMo Guardrails evaluation benchmark — prompt injection, and toxicity/safety — and the conclusion is blunt: "decision models like Jev do not reliably outperform LLM-as-a-judge, pre-trained predictive models, or open source decision models in speed or accuracy."

Prompt injection (accuracy / latency)

• Qwen3.6-35B (LLM-as-judge): 89.31% / 312.5ms
• deberta-v3-base-prompt-injection-v2: 89.01% / 54.1ms ← fastest
• DiffusionGemma-26B: 87.72% / 561.7ms
• Jev-1.13.0: 86.35% / 348.1ms
• Laya: 85.44% / 119.3ms
• → a small 2023-era purpose-built classifier is 2.7 points more accurate at one-sixth the latency

Content safety (accuracy / latency)

• Jev: 86.20% ← first place / 360.4ms
• DiffusionGemma: 85.53% / 499.3ms
• Qwen3.6-35B: 85.47% / 307.6ms
• Nemotron-3.5 (custom): 85.07% / 241.5ms
• granite-guardian-hap-125m: 80.27% / 33.2ms
• → Jev wins, but it is 11× slower than a 125M model for a 5.9-point gap

📌 How to read this benchmark: The authors concede two things. (1) "The paradigms that work well for LLM-as-a-judge are not optimal for these zero-shot classifiers" — and indeed Laya went from 57.87% to 75.20% once the policies were tuned (same model, same data). (2) Models on GPU clusters carry a minimum 56ms trans-Atlantic round trip, so their latency is not directly comparable to local CPU execution. In other words, this table is not a ranking of models; it is a record of what happens when you use them at their defaults.
📊 Credibility Assessment: 9/10
Strengths: This is the only comparison from a third party rather than a vendor that publishes its methodology, datasets, model versions and latency measurement conditions. The authors' explicit statement of their own limits (immature prompting, network latency in the numbers) adds to its credibility.
Weaknesses: The tasks are confined to two guardrail problems, so the business judgments Jev markets itself on — ticket triage, invoice processing — were not measured. It is a single run, with no variance across repeats reported.
🔗 Related Links: Red Hat independent benchmark (2026-10-02) | deberta-v3-base-prompt-injection-v2 | NeMo Guardrails

Outlook: Where the task is fixed and labeled data exists — guardrails being the clearest case — small purpose-built classifiers still win. The value of a decision model lies elsewhere: where there are no labels and the questions keep changing.

5. The Real Argument Is Calibration and Generalization, Not Accuracy

Where things stand: Line the numbers up and the category's actual fault line appears. On the typed-decisions benchmark, the fine-tuned Laya scores 0.766 accuracy against Jev's 0.727. Yet calibration error (ECE) runs 0.213 for Laya against 0.144 for Jev, and on the general intent task Banking77 the gap doubles the other way — Jev 0.870 against Laya 0.425. Decisively, Laya's zero-shot accuracy is 0.362: Laya is not a generalist but a specialist. Its own model card says performance drops outside its four target workflows.

The fine-tuned Laya in detail (model card)

• Overall: accuracy 0.766 · soft 0.471 · Brier 0.062 · ECE 0.213 · Score MAE 0.242
• By workflow: invoice processing 0.804 · security incidents 0.766 · customer service 0.764 · agent-trace observability 0.730
• By question type: noul 0.857 · choice 0.733 · score 0.723 ← weakest
• Self-admission: the probability distributions are over-confident; per-type temperatures were fitted on training data, so refit on held-out data before relying on them
• Guidance: keep choice questions under roughly 20 options

Why calibration matters more than accuracy

• Production routing works by placing a threshold on confidence
• So an over-confident 96% model is operationally worse than an honest 94% one — the threshold loses its meaning
• Accept/escalate design: the paper "JEV-as-a-Judge" (arXiv:2609.26550; Li, Miao, Krishnan, Padman) evaluates settling cases autonomously when confident and escalating to a stronger judge when not — roughly 20–40% escalate depending on the threshold
• The paper also states its own limit: threshold calibration remains task- and domain-dependent

🔍 Mind the gap between attention and usage: Jev drew 40 million views on X and the open clones passed 25. Yet Laya's typed-decisions checkpoint shows 722 downloads in the last month (HF model card, October 2026). Every metric Laya has published is Convai Innovations' own evaluation, and as of October 2026 no third-party research lab has replicated them. That is also the community's loudest objection: on r/LocalLLaMA and Hacker News the most common point was that a classifier with calibrated probabilities is a solved problem, and TypeSafe has not published enough architecture to verify the interesting claims.
📊 Credibility Assessment: 8.5/10
Strengths: All of Laya's figures and its own admissions (over-confidence, the recommendation to refit temperature, the 20-option guidance) are primary material from the official model card, as is the 722-download count. The accept/escalate design comes from the arXiv paper's text.
Weaknesses: Jev's typed-decisions accuracy splits by source between 0.727 and 67.8% (both are shown above). The benchmark itself consists of four synthetic workflows published by the Laya side, so a bias in Laya's favor cannot be ruled out.
🔗 Related Links: JEV-as-a-Judge (arXiv:2609.26550) | Laya model card (metrics and limits) | The Hacker News debate

Outlook: If this category survives, it will be decided by knowing when it might be wrong, not by how often it is right. Designs like accept/escalate hold only when probabilities mean what they say — and only then does putting a cheap judge in front of an expensive model actually pay.

Competitive Landscape and Market Positioning

A four-tier market structure

This market does not split on which model is smarter. It splits on whether you get the weights, where the thing runs, and whether it generalizes or specializes. All four tiers implement the same three question types (choice, noul, score), so in practice the choice is usually decided by deployment constraints rather than model quality.

Shifts visible in the market

• The contract standardized before the models did: Jev's /v1/systemone request shape became Ollama's default endpoint in two weeks. That is unusual speed for a category, and it turns models into swappable parts.
• The barrier to entry was proven to be effectively zero: everything from a 68M encoder (Anarkali) to methods needing no training at all (AnyJev, SemIf) satisfied the same contract. A proprietary model's only moat is zero-shot generality and honest probabilities.
• Competition dropped from quality to price, latency and placement: Jev at $0.042 per million (output free), Cloudflare Clef at $0.09 per million, Laya at $0 per token. One tier has already reached zero.
• Independent verification is cooling the hype: the first third-party benchmark, two and a half weeks after the category appeared, concluded there is no reliable advantage — and almost every published metric is a vendor self-evaluation.
• The adjacent market is real: the LLM observability and evaluation platform market is projected to grow from $2.69B in 2026 to $9.26B by 2030 (36.2% CAGR), and LLM-as-a-judge is 500–5,000× cheaper than human review while agreeing with human preference about 80% of the time. Decision models are an attempt to push that cost curve down one more step.

Application Ideas

Approaches observed in the market.

1. A like-for-like contest against the classifier you already run — pick one live classification point and compare the current approach with a decision model on the same data under the same measurement conditions. As the Red Hat results showed, the small purpose-built classifier winning is not unusual.

2. Evaluate calibration before accuracy — measure ECE and Brier first, refit temperature on held-out data, and only then set the threshold. There are model cards that admit over-confidence and recommend exactly this.

3. Design the two-stage accept/escalate path — put a cheap judge in front, settle only the confident band autonomously, and escalate the uncertain band to a stronger model or a person. Published research sees 20–40% escalate depending on the threshold.

Executive Summary

Key findings

  • A new category appeared in three weeks: TypeSafe AI released Jev on 2026-09-15 and named the "System One model" (a $40M seed led by DCVC, roughly $200M post-money, founded by RLHF co-inventor Diogo Almeida). Three days later Laya published its weights under Apache 2.0; two weeks after that, Ollama shipped /v1/systemone.
  • The output is a different kind of thing: not prose but three typed answers with probabilities — choice (up to 255 options), noul (a yes/no probability), score (a level). The category's claim is that software can use these directly as branch conditions.
  • The barrier to entry was proven to be zero: open implementations passed 25, spanning a 68M encoder, a 35B mixture-of-experts, and methods needing no training at all (AnyJev, SemIf). OpenAI, Cloudflare and Liquid AI all entered within two weeks.

Selection Guide

If the task is fixed and you have labeled data → keep the purpose-built classifier (Tier 4)
Why: under the same conditions it is more accurate at one-sixth to one-eleventh the latency. The data does not support switching to the new category.
If the questions change often and there are no labels → a hosted API such as Jev (Tier 1)
Why: usable zero-shot, 64k of context, low ECE. The costs are closed weights, data leaving your estate, and exposure to price changes.
If data sovereignty and zero marginal cost come first → an encoder model such as Laya (Tier 3)
Why: 7–33ms on CPU, $0 per token, Apache 2.0. But context is 512–1,024 tokens and it is a specialist — it only becomes useful once fine-tuned on your own data.
If you need long context and world knowledge together → Decider, Nimble or Clef (Tier 2)
Why: LLM-derived, so knowledge comes along and context runs past 8k. The price is GPUs and operational load.

Key Terminology

Decision model / System One model — A model that, instead of generating text, returns a bounded answer: one of a set of options, a score, or a yes/no probability. The name borrows from fast intuitive human judgment ("System 1"), and the central claim is that software can drop the value straight into a conditional.
choice · noul · score — The category's three question types. Choice picks one of up to 255 options, noul returns the probability a condition is true, and score returns a level on an ordered rubric. The names Jev gave them became the industry's shared vocabulary.
Calibration / ECE — Whether a model that says "90% confident" is actually right nine times out of ten. ECE (expected calibration error) measures how far off that is; lower means the probabilities can be trusted. In any system that automates on a threshold, it can matter more than accuracy.
Non-autoregressive — Producing the answer in a single computation rather than writing one word after another. Far faster because nothing is generated, but it cannot explain itself in prose. This is why Laya hits 33ms, or 7.2ms batched.
Encoder model (BERT family) — An architecture specialized in reading text and compressing its meaning, as opposed to the decoders (GPT family) that write. ModernBERT and DeBERTa are examples: small and fast, but with a short context window.
RLCD — The post-training TypeSafe says it applied to Jev, rewarding correct verdicts and usable probabilities rather than replies people prefer. The claim is that its objective differs from conversational RLHF; the method itself is unpublished.
LLM-as-a-judge — Having a large language model evaluate another model's output or a user's input. Hundreds to thousands of times cheaper than human review, but it agrees with human preference only about 80% of the time and is slow because it generates a response. This is precisely the slot decision models aim to take.
Guardrails / prompt injection — Guardrails filter an AI's inputs and outputs against a policy; prompt injection is an attack that hijacks a model's instructions through malicious input. These are the two tasks the Red Hat benchmark measured.
Accept/escalate — A two-stage design that settles cases itself when confident and hands the ambiguous ones to a stronger model or a human. It lowers total cost by putting a cheap judge first, and it only works when the probabilities are honest.
